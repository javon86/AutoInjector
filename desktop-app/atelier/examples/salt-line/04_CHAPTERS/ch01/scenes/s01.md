# One — Twelfth of May

The stove had gone out in the night, and the cottage held the particular cold of
a room that had stopped being lived in and had not yet become anything else.

Marla knelt and cleared the grate. Her father had kept kindling in a biscuit tin
under the sink and she found it there, dry, more of it than one winter needed.
He had been laying it in for a spring he did not get to.

The lighter was on the shelf above the stove where it had always been. Brass,
worn smooth on one face, a hinge that needed a thumb and a half. She struck it
and held it to the paper and watched the flame take, and only then did she turn
it over and look at what was engraved there.

**V. R.**

Her father was Thomas Vane. She had held that lighter perhaps two hundred times
in her life and had never once turned it over.

She put it in her jacket pocket rather than back on the shelf, and could not
have said why.

The box of his things was on the table where she had left it in February. She
had sorted it twice and stopped twice. On top lay the field book: canvas-backed,
1994 stamped on the spine in his own ink because the printers had got the year
wrong that season and he had refused to buy another. Thirty-one of them in the
press upstairs, one for every year he had walked the line, and this the only one
he had ever mentioned. *That was a year*, he had said, and never said what kind.

She moved it to one side, on the pile of things she was not doing this morning,
and packed instead.

The chain went in first, sixty-six feet of it, older than she was, each link
rubbed bright by hands including her own at nineteen when she had first been
allowed to carry it. Then the level. Then the stakes, eleven of them for the
south run, each one squared at the head for the date. Then her own field book,
new, the spine still stiff, nothing in it at all.

Low water was at twenty to seven. She was on the road by ten past six with the
light coming up grey off the water and the fields going by on either side.

Inland they were green. Seaward they were not.

It happened along a line you could see from the road if you knew to look for it,
and she had known since she was seven: the grass going first thin and then
yellow and then a colour that was not really a colour, and after that the crust,
white and thin and everywhere, lying in the furrows like something spilled.

Cobb's long acre came down to the shingle in a shallow bowl and the whole
seaward third of it was gone that way. She stopped at the fence without meaning
to. The wire hummed. There were no birds over the dead part, which was the thing
that always got her — not the colour, the quiet.

Two posts stood at the field's edge where she expected one.

The nearer was stake eleven, squared at the head, her father's date cut into it
last May. The other stood well inland of it, weathered pale, no survey mark on it
at all, no date, nothing. A fence post, probably. Cobb had replaced
that fence in sections over the years and left the old ones where they stood
because pulling posts is work and a post standing does no harm.

She looked at it for a moment longer than a fence post deserved, and walked on.

Kell Head at half six. She had the chain out and the level set before the water
reached its lowest, and then she did the thing she had come to do.

It was arithmetic. That was what nobody in the town understood about the survey
and what her father had never bothered to explain to them: it was not a
judgement. You took the level, you took the chain, you took the last recorded
position and you measured the difference, and the difference was the number, and
the number was where the stake went. There was no part of it that required an
opinion.

She said the figures aloud as she worked, because he had, and because there was
nobody to hear her do it.

Stake one went in at twenty past seven, four metres landward of where it had
stood the year before. She squared the head and cut the date and stood back from
it.

A stake is legal evidence if it is witnessed and dated. Hers was dated. There was
nobody within two miles to witness anything, which was lawful and which she had
not expected to mind.

She opened her own field book and wrote the first line in it in her own hand.

*12 May. Stake 1, Kell Head. −4.0 m.*

Then she looked north up the coast, the way you do when you have eleven more to
do and want to see them all at once.

She could make out Cobb's fence from where she stood, and the pale grain of the
shingle, and stake eleven standing at the edge of the long acre.

It looked further out than she remembered.

She wrote down the time instead, and picked up the chain.
