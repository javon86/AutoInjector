# Six — Two Sets of Figures

She lit the stove first, which was not necessary in the middle of May, and made
tea she did not drink, and then she sat down at the table and opened the book.

It opened like any book.

That was the part she had not prepared for. She had spent seven days walking
around this object as though it were live, and it was a canvas-backed field book
with a cracked spine and a smell of the press it had been stored in, and the
first page said *T. VANE — SURVEY — MAY 1994* in the careful capitals he used
when he expected somebody else to read it one day.

The first forty pages were a survey.

She knew how to read it in the way you know a face. His column headings, his
habit of writing the tide before the time, the small dash he put after a figure
he had taken twice. Stake by stake down the coast, Kell Head to the north end,
with the weather noted at the top of each day and the chain temperature at the
bottom because he was of a generation that still corrected for it.

She read it the way she would read any survey, which is to say she read the
numbers first.

Stake three: minus eleven point four.
Stake five: minus twenty-six point one.
Stake seven: minus nineteen point eight.
Stake nine: minus thirty-four point nine.
Stake eleven: minus forty-three point six.

She sat back.

They were not the filed figures. They were her figures — not identical, thirty
years of sea lay between them, but the same *shape*: the big landward movements
exactly where she had found the discrepancies, on Reddin's ground and Hale's and
Sowerby's two fields and Cobb's long acre. In 1994 the salt had already come that
far in. He had measured it and written it down and it was here in front of her in
his own hand.

She turned the page.

There was more survey after it. The north end, the harbour arm, the council
strip. Then the summary sheet, and the total, and the date he had finished:
14 May 1994.

Then twelve blank pages.

Then the survey again.

---

She did not understand it at first. She thought it was a fair copy — surveyors
do that, you work rough in the field and write clean afterwards — and she was
three stakes into it before the numbers stopped agreeing.

Stake three: minus three point one.
Stake five: minus four point nought.
Stake seven: minus two point eight.
Stake nine: minus five point two.
Stake eleven: minus three point six.

The open ground was identical in both. The council strip, the shingle, the
common: the same to the decimetre. Only the farms had moved.

At the bottom, in the same hand: *Survey of May 1994. Filed 3 June 1994.*

Three weeks after he finished the first one.

She got up and walked to the window and stood there for a while.

What she kept returning to, in the days afterwards, was not the fact of it. It
was the handwriting. Because the second set was *neater*. The first survey was
field-written — rain-spotted at the top corner, one figure crossed and redone,
the letters getting looser through the afternoon the way anybody's do. The
second was written at a table, unhurried, every column square, the totals ruled
twice.

He had taken more care over the false one than the true one.

She had known this man for thirty-four years. She had watched him check a
reading three times because the light had changed. And when he had sat down to
write out a set of figures he knew to be wrong, he had done it beautifully,
because he did not know how to do anything else.

---

She went through the rest of the book.

There was not much. Tide tables in the back in pencil. A note about a gate hinge.
A shopping list on the inside cover in her mother's writing, which stopped her
for a minute. Weather notes into the autumn, and then the storm, and then a gap
of eleven days with nothing at all, which she thought about for a long time.

And on the last ruled page, on its own, undated, not in survey shorthand and not
in the careful capitals either — just his ordinary hand, the one from birthday
cards:

*Two years and the ground is gone. It is not gone yet.*

That was all.

She read it perhaps twenty times. It did not become an explanation. It was not
an apology and it was not a defence and it did not name anybody, and it had been
sitting eleven miles from the harbour office in a canvas book in a press in her
father's cottage for thirty-one years.

---

Upstairs the press was where it had always been, and she opened it, and the
field books were stacked in it the way he had left them.

She counted thirty-one.

Every year he had walked the line. Every year the true survey and, after it, the
filed one. She pulled 2003 at random and found both, and 2011, and both, and the
second set always neater and always dated two or three weeks after the first.

He had kept them.

That was the thing she could not get past, standing in the cold upstairs room
with her hands full of canvas. A man covering himself burns the true figures. A
man who is ashamed burns them. Her father had measured the coast honestly every
May of his working life, written down what was actually there, filed something
else, and then carefully preserved the evidence of what he had done, in his own
house, in order, for thirty-one years, where his daughter — who would inherit the
job, because they both always knew she would — could not fail to find it.

She sat down on the floor of the press room with the books around her.

He had not left her a confession. He had left her a survey.
