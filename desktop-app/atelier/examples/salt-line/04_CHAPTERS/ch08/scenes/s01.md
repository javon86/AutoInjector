# Eight — The Formula Does Not Care

She put the sheet on the counter and Ellen Sarai read it upside down, which was
how she read everything, and did not touch it.

"Three thousand nine hundred and eighty-three point five."

"Yes."

"Against four thousand one hundred and twelve."

"Yes."

Sarai turned the sheet round with one finger and read it the right way up, which
took longer, and Marla understood that she was reading the column of eleven
figures rather than the total, checking the shape of it, the way somebody checks
whether a thing was done properly before deciding whether to be frightened of it.

"A hundred and twenty-eight and a half metres."

"A hundred and forty-one thousand three hundred and fifty pounds a year," Marla
said. "At the current rate."

"I know what it is at the current rate." Sarai put the sheet down. "I wrote the
last four grant applications."

The office was very quiet. Somebody went past outside with a dog.

"The pier's costed against the old figure," Sarai said. "That's a hundred and
sixty thousand of committed spend across three years, and about ninety of it is
already contracted. The flood insurance is written off the same number — not
the premium, the *cover*. If the defended frontage drops below four thousand the
schedule reprices. I don't know what to. Nobody's ever tested it."

"I'm not asking you to fix it."

"No, you're telling me it's coming." Sarai almost smiled. "Which is more than
your father ever did."

Marla did not say anything to that.

---

"There is a way to do this that doesn't detonate in eleven days," Sarai said.

She said it the way she said everything, flatly, as a fact available for
inspection.

"Section fourteen. If the surveyor is unable to certify the line — instrument
failure, incomplete access, a dispute over a boundary — you file provisional and
you file properly the following year. It's in the act because they knew people
get ill and boats sink. The provisional filing carries last year's figure
forward."

"That's a year."

"That's a year. The pier gets done. The insurance renews in March on the old
schedule. You walk it again next May and you file the true line and by then
you've had twelve months to tell four families what's coming instead of eleven
days." Sarai spread her hands very slightly. "And it is entirely legal. I'm not
offering you a fiddle. I'm telling you what section fourteen is for."

"Is it true, though. Am I unable to certify?"

"You've got a stake you haven't driven." Sarai looked at her. "You told me on
the fourteenth. Stake eleven, not set. That is, on the face of it, an incomplete
survey."

Marla had not driven stake eleven because she could not decide where it went, and
here was that indecision, offered back to her, wearing a legal jacket and fitting
perfectly.

"How long have you known?"

Sarai did not pretend not to understand the question.

"I've had a feeling since about 2011."

"Fifteen years."

"About that." She began squaring the papers on the counter, which she had not
needed to do. "The frontage figure moved by less than a metre a year for
nineteen years and then one year it moved by four, and I looked at it and I
thought, that's odd, and I put it in the file. And the next year it moved by
less than a metre again and I stopped thinking about it."

"You could have checked."

"I could have checked. I'd have had to borrow the theodolite from myself and
walk eleven kilometres and know how to use it, and then I'd have had to be the
woman who accused Tom Vane of something." Sarai stopped squaring the papers.
"You've met this town. I'd have needed to be right."

"And if you'd been right?"

"Then I'd have been right," Sarai said, "in 2011, and Cobb would have lost that
field fifteen years ago, and Sowerby's road access, and Reddin's strip. And I
would have had to live here afterwards."

---

Marla walked out to the van and sat in it without starting the engine.

Eleven days to the first of June. A legal instrument, correctly cited, that gave
her a year — and the year was not nothing. A year was Jenny Hale being told
before the letter arrived. A year was Cobb having a season to arrange something
for a field he was going to lose either way. Every argument for it was a decent
argument. That was the thing about it. Not one of them was self-serving.

*Two years and the ground is gone. It is not gone yet.*

She had read that line perhaps forty times and had taken it, every time, as a
sentence about the salt.

Sitting in the van outside the harbour office on the twenty-fourth of May she
read it again in her head and heard it differently, and it went through her like
cold water.

It was not about the salt.

It was about the register. Two years inside the line and the ground comes off
the book — so a man with a chain and a table and three weeks could give a field
one more year, and then next May he could give it one more year, and the field
would never be inside the line for two consecutive years, and it would never
come off the register at all.

It was not a lie he had told once in 1994 and then been trapped by.

It was a mechanism. He had found the one gap in the rule and he had put his whole
working life through it, one year at a time, thirty-one times, and every year he
had written down the truth first so he would know exactly how much he was
holding back.

Section fourteen was the same shape. Smaller, legal, and the same shape.

She started the engine.
