# Four — The Pattern

The shingle between Kell Head and Sanders Point was six kilometres of loose
grey stone and it went under at high water, so you crossed it in the four hours
either side of the low or you did not cross it. On the sixteenth the window
opened at ten to eight and shut at ten to twelve, and the work she meant to do
out there was six hours long.

She could have hurried it. She thought about that on the walk out, the
theodolite in its case banging against her hip, and decided she would rather do
half of it properly and come back for the rest than do all of it fast.

Her father would have hurried it. She was fairly sure of that and did not want
to be.

The other thing she had decided, and had decided by not doing it, was that she
was not going to open the 1994 field book. It had sat on the table through
Tuesday and Wednesday and it was sitting there now. She had walked past it four
times that morning. There was a version of this where she made herself a cup of
tea and opened it and knew by nine o'clock, and she had chosen instead to walk
six kilometres over stones carrying an instrument that weighed as much as a
child.

She knew what that was. She kept walking.

Stake two was true.

She read it twice because she had not expected it and had built no room in
herself for it to be true. Minus one point eight metres from the 1994 recorded
position, which was ordinary, which was thirty years of ordinary sea.

Stake three was out by eight metres.

Stake four was true.

Stake five was out by twenty-two.

She sat down on the shingle at the top of the bank after stake five with the
field book on her knees and looked at the four figures and felt the thing turn
over in her chest that she had been keeping down since Tuesday.

Because random error does not do that.

If she had been wrong — if the instrument were sick, if her method had a flaw in
it, if she were simply not as good as she believed — the error would be
everywhere. It would be in stake two and stake four. It would drift with the
weather and the ground and it would be small and it would be *dull*, the way real
error is dull.

This was not dull. This was two true, one out, one true, one badly out.

She worked on. She stopped hoping and started predicting, which she recognised
even at the time as the moment the day changed. Before stake seven she wrote in
the margin, *expect seaward*, and stake seven was seaward, by fourteen.

Stake six: true.

Stake eight: true.

Stake nine: out by thirty-one.

The tide turned at ten to twelve and she came back over the shingle with the
water coming up behind her along the bar the way it does, not fast, just
without any interest in whether you have finished.

At the van she took out a clean sheet and drew the coast.

She was not good at drawing and it did not matter. Kell Head at the bottom, the
long bay, Sanders Point at the top, the road running behind. She marked the
eleven positions she had. She put a circle round the four that were wrong:
three, five, seven, nine — and eleven, which she had not driven, which made five.

Then she sat and looked at it for a long moment, because she had drawn the farm
boundaries in without deciding to, the way you do when you have known a coast
since you were seven.

The five wrong stakes were not scattered along the line.

They were where the line crossed onto somebody's ground.

Stake three was Reddin's. Stake five was Hale's. Seven and nine were Sowerby's
two fields, which is why there were two. Eleven was Cobb's long acre.

Every stake that stood on open ground, on the common, on the shingle, on the
council strip behind the sea wall, was true to within two metres.

Every stake that stood on a farm was seaward.

She put the pencil down.

It was ten past two in the afternoon and the light was flat and there was salt
drying white on the backs of her hands from the crossing, and she understood
that she was no longer looking at an error. An error does not know where the
fences are.

---

She drove home the long way, which was not the long way by much, and she was
aware that she was doing it.

Cobb. Reddin. Hale. Sowerby.

She had known all four families her whole life. Reddin had taught her to swim,
badly, off the slipway. Hale's daughter had been in her class. Sowerby was
dead now and the two fields were his son's, and the son had a boy of his own
who would be seven or eight.

Four farms. And she went through it three times before it came, and when it came
it was not a revelation so much as a thing sliding into a slot it had been cut
for.

They were the four with land below the condemnation level.

The rule she had grown up hearing discussed like weather. Two years inside the
line and the ground came off the agricultural register, and there was
compensation, and the compensation was not enough.

Four farms had ground below that level in 1995.

Four farms had a stake standing seaward of where it should be.

She drove past the turn for the cottage without meaning to, and had to go up as
far as the chapel to turn round, and came back down and pulled in and sat with
the engine off.

Through the window she could see the table, and the box, and on top of it the
canvas-backed book with 1994 written on the spine in her father's ink.
