# One — Twelfth of May

The stove had gone out in the night, and the cottage held the particular cold of
a room that had stopped being lived in and had not yet become anything else.

Marla knelt and cleared the grate. Her father had kept kindling in a biscuit tin
under the sink and she found it there, dry, more of it than one winter needed.
He had been laying it in for a spring he did not get to.

The lighter was on the shelf above the stove where it had always been. Brass,
worn smooth on one face, a hinge that needed a thumb and a half. She struck it
and held it to the paper and watched the flame take, and only then did she turn
it over and look at what was engraved there.

**V. R.**

Her father was Thomas Vane. She had held that lighter perhaps two hundred times
in her life and had never once turned it over.

She put it in her jacket pocket rather than back on the shelf, and could not
have said why.

The box of his things was on the table where she had left it in February. She
had sorted it twice and stopped twice. On top lay the field book: canvas-backed,
1994 stamped on the spine in his own ink because the printers had got the year
wrong that season and he had refused to buy another. Thirty-one of them in the
press upstairs, one for every year he had walked the line, and this the only one
he had ever mentioned. *That was a year*, he had said, and never said what kind.

She moved it to one side, on the pile of things she was not doing this morning,
and packed instead.

The chain went in first, sixty-six feet of it, older than she was, each link
rubbed bright by hands including her own at nineteen when she had first been
allowed to carry it. Then the level. Then the stakes, eleven of them for the
south run, each one squared at the head for the date. Then her own field book,
new, the spine still stiff, nothing in it at all.

Low water was at twenty to seven. She was on the road by ten past six with the
light coming up grey off the water and the fields going by on either side.

Inland they were green. Seaward they were not.

It happened along a line you could see from the road if you knew to look for it,
and she had known since she was seven: the grass going first thin and then
yellow and then a colour that was not really a colour, and after that the crust,
white and thin and everywhere, lying in the furrows like something spilled.

Cobb's long acre came down to the shingle in a shallow bowl and the whole
seaward third of it was gone that way. She stopped at the fence without meaning
to. The wire hummed. There were no birds over the dead part, which was the thing
that always got her — not the colour, the quiet.

Two posts stood at the field's edge where she expected one.

The nearer was stake eleven, squared at the head, her father's date cut into it
last May. The other stood well inland of it, weathered pale, no survey mark on it
at all, no date, nothing. A fence post, probably. Cobb had replaced
that fence in sections over the years and left the old ones where they stood
because pulling posts is work and a post standing does no harm.

She looked at it for a moment longer than a fence post deserved, and walked on.

Kell Head at half six. She had the chain out and the level set before the water
reached its lowest, and then she did the thing she had come to do.

It was arithmetic. That was what nobody in the town understood about the survey
and what her father had never bothered to explain to them: it was not a
judgement. You took the level, you took the chain, you took the last recorded
position and you measured the difference, and the difference was the number, and
the number was where the stake went. There was no part of it that required an
opinion.

She said the figures aloud as she worked, because he had, and because there was
nobody to hear her do it.

Stake one went in at twenty past seven, four metres landward of where it had
stood the year before. She squared the head and cut the date and stood back from
it.

A stake is legal evidence if it is witnessed and dated. Hers was dated. There was
nobody within two miles to witness anything, which was lawful and which she had
not expected to mind.

She opened her own field book and wrote the first line in it in her own hand.

*12 May. Stake 1, Kell Head. −4.0 m.*

Then she looked north up the coast, the way you do when you have eleven more to
do and want to see them all at once.

She could make out Cobb's fence from where she stood, and the pale grain of the
shingle, and stake eleven standing at the edge of the long acre.

It looked further out than she remembered.

She wrote down the time instead, and picked up the chain.

# Two — Stake Eleven

Stakes eight, nine and ten went in before eight o'clock and none of them gave her
any trouble.

Eight moved two metres landward. Nine moved two and a half. Ten moved one, which
was less than she expected and meant nothing on its own; the shingle bar out from
Sanders Point took the worst of the weather off that stretch and always had.

She liked the work. That was the part she had never found a way to say to
anyone without it sounding either pious or simple. You put the chain down and
you read what was there and the ground did not care what you wanted, and at the
end of it you had a number that was true whether or not anybody liked it. There
was very little else in her life she could say that about.

By ten past eight she was at the top of the long acre with the chain laid out
and stake eleven's position marked from the year before, and she ran the
measurement, and it came out wrong.

Not wrong the way a figure is wrong when your hand slips. Wrong by forty metres.

She stood for a moment with the field book open and the wind coming up the bowl
off the shingle and read it again.

Then she coiled the chain and did the whole thing a second time, from the datum
at the top of the field, working down, saying the figures aloud.

Forty-one point two.

The stake in front of her — squared head, her father's date cut into it last
May — stood forty-one metres seaward of where the recorded line said the salt
line had been in 1994. That was not a correction. That was not a year's drift or
five years' drift. The line only moved one way and it moved slowly and thirty
years of it had never added up to forty metres anywhere on this coast.

She checked the level against the benchmark on the sea wall, which was cut into
granite in 1911 and had not moved since. The level was sound.

She checked the chain against its own marks. Sound.

She sat down on the crust at the edge of the dead ground, which broke under her
like the top of a cake, and worked the arithmetic on paper in a way she had not
needed to do since she was twenty.

It held.

There were three possibilities and she made herself list them in order of how
much she wanted them to be true.

The first was that she had made an error, twice, in two different ways, using
two instruments, on the second day of a survey she had trained eleven years for.

The second was that the recorded 1994 position was wrong — a transcription slip,
a figure carried over badly, the sort of thing that happens once in a long
career and gets found and corrected the next spring by whoever walks the line.

The third was that the stake was where somebody had put it.

She did not write the third one down.

What she did think, and did not write either, was that forty metres was not an
abstract quantity on that particular field. The condemnation rule had come in
when she was three and everyone on the coast could recite it: ground inside the
line two years running came off the agricultural register, and the compensation
was not enough, and everybody knew the compensation was not enough. Forty metres
of the long acre was the difference between a field and a payment.

She had grown up hearing it discussed the way people discuss weather they cannot
change.

What she wrote down was this: *13 May. Stake 11, long acre. Chain reads −41.2 m
from 1994 recorded. Stake as set 1995–2025 stands +40 m seaward of chain
position. Not driven. See over.*

And then, because she was her father's daughter and he had taught her that a
survey is only worth what its notes are worth, she turned the page and wrote the
weather, the tide, the time, the instrument serials, and the fact that she had
run the measurement twice from an independent datum.

She did not drive stake eleven. It stood there behind her, last year's, dated in
his hand.

She had been at it long enough that she saw Cobb coming a good while before he
arrived, down the track with his shoulders going and the list in his walk from
the hip that set badly in 1998. He stopped a courteous distance off, the way men
of his generation did with a woman working, and looked at the chain rather than
at her.

"You've the good weather for it."

"I have."

"He always said the second week." Cobb nodded at nothing in particular. "Never
the first. First week the ground's still lying to you, he said."

"He said that to me too."

They stood in it for a moment. Out on the shingle a gull went over and did not
call.

"The wire's rotten along the bottom there." Cobb tipped his head at the fence.
"I'll get to it."

"It's not my business what the wire does."

"No." He almost smiled. "It never was his either. He'd tell me about it all the
same."

She waited. She had learned that from her father as well, though he had never
taught it deliberately: that if you say nothing for slightly longer than is
comfortable, most people will fill it in for you.

Cobb looked at the chain again, and then at the ground where it lay, and then at
the stake standing forty metres out with last year's date on it.

"Which year's figures are you working from?"

He said it lightly. He said it the way you ask what time the bus goes.

Marla closed her field book with her thumb inside it, which she had not decided
to do.

"I've not finished yet."

"No," Cobb said. "Take your time."

He went back up the track. She watched him go, and then she did one more thing
before she packed the chain, which she could not have justified if anybody had
asked.

She measured from stake eleven to the old unmarked post standing further inland
— the weathered one, no survey mark, that she had noticed the morning before and
decided was a fence post.

Forty metres. Give or take the width of her hand.

# Three — The Second Reading

The harbour board kept a theodolite in a cupboard in the back office and had
done since before Marla was born, on the theory that a coastal authority ought
to own an instrument. It came out perhaps twice a decade. Ellen Sarai found the
key on the third try and set the case on the counter between them and opened the
log.

"Purpose of use."

"Verification."

Sarai waited with the pen.

"Verification, stake eleven," Marla said.

Sarai wrote it down in a hand that had been trained on forms and turned the log
round for her signature, and Marla signed it, and that was the moment it stopped
being a private uncertainty and became a thing with a date on it in a book kept
by somebody else.

"Filing's the first of June," Sarai said, not looking up.

"I know when the filing is."

"I'm not suggesting you don't." Sarai closed the log. "I'm saying it out loud so
that later on neither of us has to pretend I didn't."

The theodolite was heavier than she expected and she carried it out to the van
with both arms, the way you carry something that is not yours.

---

She set it up at the top of the long acre at half eight with the tide going out
and did the whole thing cold.

That was the discipline and she made herself keep to it: she did not look at
yesterday's figure, did not open the field book to the page, did not let herself
form a number before the instrument gave her one. She set the datum from the
granite benchmark on the sea wall, cut in 1911, which had outlasted two harbours
and every surveyor who had ever used it. She ran the traverse down the field.
She read it off.

Forty-one point one.

She sat back on her heels.

Two instruments. Three readings. Two datums, one of them a piece of Victorian
granite that had not moved in a hundred and fourteen years. Whatever else was
true, the number was not coming out of her hands.

Cobb was at the top of the track. He had been there some while, she thought; he
had the stillness of a man who has decided not to be noticed arriving.

"I need a witness," she said.

He came down. He looked at the theodolite for a long moment, and at the field
book open on the case, and at stake eleven standing out in the dead ground with
last year's date cut into its head and no new stake beside it.

"For what."

"For a reading. It's not evidence unless somebody watches me take it and puts
their name to what they watched."

"And you want mine."

"You're the only person within two miles."

That was true and it was also not the whole truth and they both knew it. The
long acre was his. A reading taken on his ground, witnessed by him, was worth
more than a reading witnessed by a stranger, because nobody could say afterwards
that the owner had not been there to see it done.

Cobb stood with his hands at his sides.

He was slow about it. Not reluctant exactly — she had known reluctance and this
was something else, something more like a man doing arithmetic of his own. She
watched him look out at the stake and then at the fence and then at the dead
third of his field with the crust lying in the furrows, and she understood, in a
way she could not have put into a sentence, that he was not deciding whether to
sign. He was deciding what signing would cost, and then he was going to sign
anyway.

"Show me where," he said.

She ran the reading again with him standing at her shoulder, and said each figure
aloud as she took it, and wrote it, and turned the book round.

He read it slowly, the whole page, the way a man reads something he intends to
be held to.

Then he took the pen and wrote *Ivor Cobb*, and under it, unprompted, *witnessed
14 May, own ground*, which was more than she had asked for and more than the law
required, and which she would think about a great deal later.

---

They walked up the track together afterwards, her carrying the theodolite, him
carrying the case because he had taken it out of her hand without asking.

"Ivor," she said. "What happened in 1994?"

He did not break stride.

"Bad year. You'd not remember it, you'd have been what — two?"

"Two."

"There was the storm in the November. Took the roof off the net store and put
three foot of water through the low road, and after it the fields came up wrong
in the spring. Salt in them that hadn't been. Everybody's, not just mine." He
shifted the case to his other hand. "Your father walked the line that May and he
was a long time about it. Longer than he'd ever been."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Cobb agreed.

They came up onto the road. The wind had swung round and was coming off the land
now, which meant weather later.

"He was a good man," Cobb said, "and I'll not have it said otherwise in front of
me," and there was nothing in his voice that was warning her, and she understood
that she had just been warned.

She let it stand. That was the thing she noticed about herself, walking back to
the van: that she had asked one direct question in her life and had let the
answer go by, and that she had done it because she was afraid of what a second
question would do to a man who had just signed his name to a number that would
cost him his field.

The 1994 field book was on the table in the cottage where she had left it on the
twelfth, canvas-backed, the year in his own ink.

She thought about it the whole way back and did not turn the van around.

# Four — The Pattern

The shingle between Kell Head and Sanders Point was six kilometres of loose
grey stone and it went under at high water, so you crossed it in the four hours
either side of the low or you did not cross it. On the sixteenth the window
opened at ten to eight and shut at ten to twelve, and the work she meant to do
out there was six hours long.

She could have hurried it. She thought about that on the walk out, the
theodolite in its case banging against her hip, and decided she would rather do
half of it properly and come back for the rest than do all of it fast.

Her father would have hurried it. She was fairly sure of that and did not want
to be.

The other thing she had decided, and had decided by not doing it, was that she
was not going to open the 1994 field book. It had sat on the table through
Tuesday and Wednesday and it was sitting there now. She had walked past it four
times that morning. There was a version of this where she made herself a cup of
tea and opened it and knew by nine o'clock, and she had chosen instead to walk
six kilometres over stones carrying an instrument that weighed as much as a
child.

She knew what that was. She kept walking.

Stake two was true.

She read it twice because she had not expected it and had built no room in
herself for it to be true. Minus one point eight metres from the 1994 recorded
position, which was ordinary, which was thirty years of ordinary sea.

Stake three was out by eight metres.

Stake four was true.

Stake five was out by twenty-two.

She sat down on the shingle at the top of the bank after stake five with the
field book on her knees and looked at the four figures and felt the thing turn
over in her chest that she had been keeping down since Tuesday.

Because random error does not do that.

If she had been wrong — if the instrument were sick, if her method had a flaw in
it, if she were simply not as good as she believed — the error would be
everywhere. It would be in stake two and stake four. It would drift with the
weather and the ground and it would be small and it would be *dull*, the way real
error is dull.

This was not dull. This was two true, one out, one true, one badly out.

She worked on. She stopped hoping and started predicting, which she recognised
even at the time as the moment the day changed. Before stake seven she wrote in
the margin, *expect seaward*, and stake seven was seaward, by fourteen.

Stake six: true.

Stake eight: true.

Stake nine: out by thirty-one.

The tide turned at ten to twelve and she came back over the shingle with the
water coming up behind her along the bar the way it does, not fast, just
without any interest in whether you have finished.

At the van she took out a clean sheet and drew the coast.

She was not good at drawing and it did not matter. Kell Head at the bottom, the
long bay, Sanders Point at the top, the road running behind. She marked the
eleven positions she had. She put a circle round the four that were wrong:
three, five, seven, nine — and eleven, which she had not driven, which made five.

Then she sat and looked at it for a long moment, because she had drawn the farm
boundaries in without deciding to, the way you do when you have known a coast
since you were seven.

The five wrong stakes were not scattered along the line.

They were where the line crossed onto somebody's ground.

Stake three was Reddin's. Stake five was Hale's. Seven and nine were Sowerby's
two fields, which is why there were two. Eleven was Cobb's long acre.

Every stake that stood on open ground, on the common, on the shingle, on the
council strip behind the sea wall, was true to within two metres.

Every stake that stood on a farm was seaward.

She put the pencil down.

It was ten past two in the afternoon and the light was flat and there was salt
drying white on the backs of her hands from the crossing, and she understood
that she was no longer looking at an error. An error does not know where the
fences are.

---

She drove home the long way, which was not the long way by much, and she was
aware that she was doing it.

Cobb. Reddin. Hale. Sowerby.

She had known all four families her whole life. Reddin had taught her to swim,
badly, off the slipway. Hale's daughter had been in her class. Sowerby was
dead now and the two fields were his son's, and the son had a boy of his own
who would be seven or eight.

Four farms. And she went through it three times before it came, and when it came
it was not a revelation so much as a thing sliding into a slot it had been cut
for.

They were the four with land below the condemnation level.

The rule she had grown up hearing discussed like weather. Two years inside the
line and the ground came off the agricultural register, and there was
compensation, and the compensation was not enough.

Four farms had ground below that level in 1995.

Four farms had a stake standing seaward of where it should be.

She drove past the turn for the cottage without meaning to, and had to go up as
far as the chapel to turn round, and came back down and pulled in and sat with
the engine off.

Through the window she could see the table, and the box, and on top of it the
canvas-backed book with 1994 written on the spine in her father's ink.

# Five — What the Town Assumes

The theodolite went back in its cupboard and Ellen Sarai opened the log to the
same page and turned it round.

"Outcome of verification."

Marla looked at the box. It was two inches wide and ruled in blue.

She could put down what had happened. *Reading confirmed: stake 11 stands 40.0 m
seaward of the 1994 recorded position. Four further stakes similarly displaced.
Displacement occurs only where the line crosses private agricultural ground.*
It would fit if she wrote small. It would be in a book kept by the harbour
board, in a building with a door that locked, and it would be a public document
from the moment the ink dried.

She wrote *Reading confirmed.*

Sarai read it upside down, which she did with everything, and said nothing at
all, and closed the log.

"You'll have it in by the first."

"That's the date."

"It is." Sarai went round the counter and began the business of putting the key
back on the board, which took longer than it needed to. "You know how the grant
works?"

"Roughly."

"You don't, then." Not unkind. "The coastal maintenance grant is calculated off
the filed line. Not off the harbour, not off the population — off the line. It's
a formula. Length of defended frontage times a rate, and defended frontage means
everything seaward of the salt line, because that's the ground the wall is
holding."

Marla stood very still.

"So every metre the line moves landward," Sarai said, "is frontage that stops
being defended, on paper. Which is money that stops arriving. It's about
eleven hundred a metre, over the whole run, in the current formula."

"Eleven hundred."

"Give or take. It's a bad formula. Everyone knows it's a bad formula. It was
written in 1988 by somebody who had never seen this coast and it has survived
four reviews because nobody has an appetite for opening it." Sarai shut the
cupboard. "Last year's filed figure was four thousand one hundred and twelve
metres of defended frontage. That's what the budget's built on. That's the
number the pier repair is costed against and the number in the flood insurance
schedule."

She said it the way you say a thing you have said a hundred times, as an
assumed fact, as the baseline everybody works from.

Four thousand one hundred and twelve.

Marla had, in her jacket pocket, folded twice, a sheet of paper with the coast
drawn on it and five circles marked, and she knew — not exactly, not yet, but
within a hundred metres either way — that the true figure was something in the
region of three thousand nine hundred.

Two hundred metres. Two hundred and twenty thousand pounds a year.

She opened her mouth and did not say it.

"Right," Sarai said. "Well. First of June."

"First of June," Marla said, and went out into the street.

---

She had meant to buy bread and go home and instead she was standing outside the
bakery being talked to by Jenny Hale, who had been two desks over for eleven
years and who now had a child on her hip and the specific tiredness of somebody
who has been up since five.

"I heard you were doing the line."

"I am."

"Dad'll be glad." Jenny shifted the child. "He was in a state about it, honestly
— he thought they'd send someone. From the council. Some lad with a tablet who'd
never walked it, being strict about it."

"Strict about it," Marla said.

"You know what I mean." Jenny laughed. "Your dad knew the ground. That's all Dad
means. Someone who knows what the ground actually does, not just what the book
says."

The child put its hand flat on Jenny's collarbone and looked at Marla with the
frank uninterest of the very young.

"He's got the wet corner in barley this year," Jenny said. "First time since I
don't know when. He's dead pleased with himself."

Marla said that was good, and that she'd tell him when she saw him, and bought
the bread, and drove home.

*Strict about it.*

It sat with her the whole way. Because the opposite of strict, in this town, in
that sentence, said fondly by a woman holding a baby outside a bakery, was not
*corrupt*. It was not *dishonest*. The opposite of strict was *decent*. It was
*one of us*. It was *a man who knew what the ground actually does*.

Her father had been called that his whole life and she had always heard it as a
compliment about his competence.

---

At the cottage she wrote up the day, because he had taught her that a survey is
only worth what its notes are worth, and she got as far as the harbour office
and stopped.

*18 May. Instrument returned. Log entry: reading confirmed.*

She looked at that for a while.

What had happened this morning was that a woman had said a false number out loud
in an official building, as the basis of a budget, and Marla had known it was
false, and had said nothing, and had walked out into the sunshine and bought
bread.

Nothing had happened. No alarm. No moment. Sarai had put the key back on the
board and said first of June and that had been the whole of it.

It had taken no effort at all. That was the part she kept coming back to. She had
imagined — in some vague way she had never examined — that a lie of that kind
would require something of a person. Nerve, or a plan, or at minimum a
sensation.

It had required her to stand still for four seconds.

Thirty years, she thought. Thirty years is not one enormous act. Thirty years is
this, four seconds at a time, in a building with a door that locks, every May,
by a man everybody called decent.

She sat at the table for a long while with her hand flat on the canvas cover of
the 1994 field book.

Then she took her hand off it and went to bed.

# Six — Two Sets of Figures

She lit the stove first, which was not necessary in the middle of May, and made
tea she did not drink, and then she sat down at the table and opened the book.

It opened like any book.

That was the part she had not prepared for. She had spent seven days walking
around this object as though it were live, and it was a canvas-backed field book
with a cracked spine and a smell of the press it had been stored in, and the
first page said *T. VANE — SURVEY — MAY 1994* in the careful capitals he used
when he expected somebody else to read it one day.

The first forty pages were a survey.

She knew how to read it in the way you know a face. His column headings, his
habit of writing the tide before the time, the small dash he put after a figure
he had taken twice. Stake by stake down the coast, Kell Head to the north end,
with the weather noted at the top of each day and the chain temperature at the
bottom because he was of a generation that still corrected for it.

She read it the way she would read any survey, which is to say she read the
numbers first.

Stake three: minus eleven point four.
Stake five: minus twenty-six point one.
Stake seven: minus nineteen point eight.
Stake nine: minus thirty-four point nine.
Stake eleven: minus forty-three point six.

She sat back.

They were not the filed figures. They were her figures — not identical, thirty
years of sea lay between them, but the same *shape*: the big landward movements
exactly where she had found the discrepancies, on Reddin's ground and Hale's and
Sowerby's two fields and Cobb's long acre. In 1994 the salt had already come that
far in. He had measured it and written it down and it was here in front of her in
his own hand.

She turned the page.

There was more survey after it. The north end, the harbour arm, the council
strip. Then the summary sheet, and the total, and the date he had finished:
14 May 1994.

Then twelve blank pages.

Then the survey again.

---

She did not understand it at first. She thought it was a fair copy — surveyors
do that, you work rough in the field and write clean afterwards — and she was
three stakes into it before the numbers stopped agreeing.

Stake three: minus three point one.
Stake five: minus four point nought.
Stake seven: minus two point eight.
Stake nine: minus five point two.
Stake eleven: minus three point six.

The open ground was identical in both. The council strip, the shingle, the
common: the same to the decimetre. Only the farms had moved.

At the bottom, in the same hand: *Survey of May 1994. Filed 3 June 1994.*

Three weeks after he finished the first one.

She got up and walked to the window and stood there for a while.

What she kept returning to, in the days afterwards, was not the fact of it. It
was the handwriting. Because the second set was *neater*. The first survey was
field-written — rain-spotted at the top corner, one figure crossed and redone,
the letters getting looser through the afternoon the way anybody's do. The
second was written at a table, unhurried, every column square, the totals ruled
twice.

He had taken more care over the false one than the true one.

She had known this man for thirty-four years. She had watched him check a
reading three times because the light had changed. And when he had sat down to
write out a set of figures he knew to be wrong, he had done it beautifully,
because he did not know how to do anything else.

---

She went through the rest of the book.

There was not much. Tide tables in the back in pencil. A note about a gate hinge.
A shopping list on the inside cover in her mother's writing, which stopped her
for a minute. Weather notes into the autumn, and then the storm, and then a gap
of eleven days with nothing at all, which she thought about for a long time.

And on the last ruled page, on its own, undated, not in survey shorthand and not
in the careful capitals either — just his ordinary hand, the one from birthday
cards:

*Two years and the ground is gone. It is not gone yet.*

That was all.

She read it perhaps twenty times. It did not become an explanation. It was not
an apology and it was not a defence and it did not name anybody, and it had been
sitting eleven miles from the harbour office in a canvas book in a press in her
father's cottage for thirty-one years.

---

Upstairs the press was where it had always been, and she opened it, and the
field books were stacked in it the way he had left them.

She counted thirty-one.

Every year he had walked the line. Every year the true survey and, after it, the
filed one. She pulled 2003 at random and found both, and 2011, and both, and the
second set always neater and always dated two or three weeks after the first.

He had kept them.

That was the thing she could not get past, standing in the cold upstairs room
with her hands full of canvas. A man covering himself burns the true figures. A
man who is ashamed burns them. Her father had measured the coast honestly every
May of his working life, written down what was actually there, filed something
else, and then carefully preserved the evidence of what he had done, in his own
house, in order, for thirty-one years, where his daughter — who would inherit the
job, because they both always knew she would — could not fail to find it.

She sat down on the floor of the press room with the books around her.

He had not left her a confession. He had left her a survey.

# Seven — The Total

It took three days.

She walked it from Kell Head to the north end and back, and she did every stake
cold, and she wrote each one down before she let herself think about the one
before it. Twenty-first, twenty-second, twenty-third of May. The weather held,
which she was grateful for and did not trust.

Stake one: minus four point nought.
Two: minus one point eight.
Three: minus eight point nought.
Four: minus two point nought.
Five: minus twenty-two.
Six: minus one point nought.
Seven: minus fourteen.
Eight: minus two point five.
Nine: minus thirty-one.
Ten: minus one point nought.
Eleven: minus forty-one point two.

On the evening of the twenty-third she sat at the table with the stove going and
did the totalling, which is the part of a survey nobody outside it has any
picture of. It is not dramatic. It is a column of figures and a rule and a great
deal of checking, and she did it twice with a gap in between and got the same
answer both times.

The filed frontage was four thousand one hundred and twelve metres.

The true frontage was three thousand nine hundred and eighty-three point five.

One hundred and twenty-eight point five metres.

She looked at that for a while.

Because she had told herself two hundred. Standing in the harbour office on the
eighteenth with Sarai saying *four thousand one hundred and twelve* like a thing
everyone knew, she had done the arithmetic in her head off five stakes and a
guess and come out at two hundred metres and two hundred and twenty thousand
pounds a year, and she had carried that number around for five days.

It was a hundred and twenty-eight and a half. About a hundred and forty-one
thousand.

Less than two thirds of what she had been dreading.

And she sat there and understood, with the particular clarity you get at the end
of three days' walking when you are too tired to lie to yourself, that she was
disappointed.

That was the shape of it. Not relieved. Disappointed. Some part of her had wanted
the number to be enormous — wanted it catastrophic, unarguable, a figure so
large that publishing it would stop being a decision and become an obligation.
Two hundred thousand pounds and four ruined farms is a tragedy that happens to
you. A hundred and forty-one thousand is a sum. Sums have to be weighed against
other things. Somebody has to choose.

She had wanted to be relieved of the choosing.

---

The other totalling took longer and she did it the next morning with the coast
drawn out again and the true positions marked.

The condemnation rule did not care about frontage. It cared about ground: two
consecutive years inside the line and the register lets it go.

She went along the eleven and worked out what the true line actually enclosed.

Reddin: the seaward strip below the road. Eleven acres, poor ground, grazed not
cropped. It went inside the line by six metres. Reddin would lose eleven acres he
had not made money on in a decade and would mind terribly, because it had been
his father's.

Hale: nothing. The true line ran four metres seaward of Hale's boundary at every
point she could measure. Hale's wet corner, in barley this year for the first
time in years, that Jenny had been so pleased about — it stayed. She checked it
three times.

Sowerby's two fields: the near one stayed. The far one went, entirely — nine
acres, and it was the nine that had the road access, which meant the near one
became a field you could only reach across somebody else's ground.

Cobb: the long acre went. All of it. Not the dead third that anybody standing at
the fence could see was finished — the whole field, because the true line ran
along the top hedge where the old unmarked post had been standing since 1994,
saying so, in plain sight, for thirty-one years.

Two farms losing ground outright. One losing the use of what it kept. One
untouched.

Four families, and she could put a name to what each of them lost, and one of
them was the man who had signed his name to her reading on his own ground because
she asked him to.

---

She walked down to the long acre on the evening of the twenty-fourth, not to
measure anything.

The wire was still rotten along the bottom. The crust lay in the furrows. Stake
eleven stood out in the dead part with last year's date on it and no new stake
beside it, because she still had not driven one, and forty metres inland the old
post stood where it had always stood.

She put her hand on it. It was soft on the weather side and hard underneath, the
way a post gets.

Thirty-one years. A man had stood here in the May of 1994 with a chain and this
post and the true figure in his book, and had walked back up that track, and had
sat down three weeks later at a table and written out something else.

She did not hear the truck. She heard it stop.

Cobb was at the top of the track with the engine off and the window down, and he
looked at her standing at the old post with her hand on it, and she looked back
at him, and neither of them said anything at all.

Then he put the truck in gear and reversed up the track and away, which he had
never once done in her life.

# Eight — The Formula Does Not Care

She put the sheet on the counter and Ellen Sarai read it upside down, which was
how she read everything, and did not touch it.

"Three thousand nine hundred and eighty-three point five."

"Yes."

"Against four thousand one hundred and twelve."

"Yes."

Sarai turned the sheet round with one finger and read it the right way up, which
took longer, and Marla understood that she was reading the column of eleven
figures rather than the total, checking the shape of it, the way somebody checks
whether a thing was done properly before deciding whether to be frightened of it.

"A hundred and twenty-eight and a half metres."

"A hundred and forty-one thousand three hundred and fifty pounds a year," Marla
said. "At the current rate."

"I know what it is at the current rate." Sarai put the sheet down. "I wrote the
last four grant applications."

The office was very quiet. Somebody went past outside with a dog.

"The pier's costed against the old figure," Sarai said. "That's a hundred and
sixty thousand of committed spend across three years, and about ninety of it is
already contracted. The flood insurance is written off the same number — not
the premium, the *cover*. If the defended frontage drops below four thousand the
schedule reprices. I don't know what to. Nobody's ever tested it."

"I'm not asking you to fix it."

"No, you're telling me it's coming." Sarai almost smiled. "Which is more than
your father ever did."

Marla did not say anything to that.

---

"There is a way to do this that doesn't detonate in eleven days," Sarai said.

She said it the way she said everything, flatly, as a fact available for
inspection.

"Section fourteen. If the surveyor is unable to certify the line — instrument
failure, incomplete access, a dispute over a boundary — you file provisional and
you file properly the following year. It's in the act because they knew people
get ill and boats sink. The provisional filing carries last year's figure
forward."

"That's a year."

"That's a year. The pier gets done. The insurance renews in March on the old
schedule. You walk it again next May and you file the true line and by then
you've had twelve months to tell four families what's coming instead of eleven
days." Sarai spread her hands very slightly. "And it is entirely legal. I'm not
offering you a fiddle. I'm telling you what section fourteen is for."

"Is it true, though. Am I unable to certify?"

"You've got a stake you haven't driven." Sarai looked at her. "You told me on
the fourteenth. Stake eleven, not set. That is, on the face of it, an incomplete
survey."

Marla had not driven stake eleven because she could not decide where it went, and
here was that indecision, offered back to her, wearing a legal jacket and fitting
perfectly.

"How long have you known?"

Sarai did not pretend not to understand the question.

"I've had a feeling since about 2011."

"Fifteen years."

"About that." She began squaring the papers on the counter, which she had not
needed to do. "The frontage figure moved by less than a metre a year for
nineteen years and then one year it moved by four, and I looked at it and I
thought, that's odd, and I put it in the file. And the next year it moved by
less than a metre again and I stopped thinking about it."

"You could have checked."

"I could have checked. I'd have had to borrow the theodolite from myself and
walk eleven kilometres and know how to use it, and then I'd have had to be the
woman who accused Tom Vane of something." Sarai stopped squaring the papers.
"You've met this town. I'd have needed to be right."

"And if you'd been right?"

"Then I'd have been right," Sarai said, "in 2011, and Cobb would have lost that
field fifteen years ago, and Sowerby's road access, and Reddin's strip. And I
would have had to live here afterwards."

---

Marla walked out to the van and sat in it without starting the engine.

Eleven days to the first of June. A legal instrument, correctly cited, that gave
her a year — and the year was not nothing. A year was Jenny Hale being told
before the letter arrived. A year was Cobb having a season to arrange something
for a field he was going to lose either way. Every argument for it was a decent
argument. That was the thing about it. Not one of them was self-serving.

*Two years and the ground is gone. It is not gone yet.*

She had read that line perhaps forty times and had taken it, every time, as a
sentence about the salt.

Sitting in the van outside the harbour office on the twenty-fourth of May she
read it again in her head and heard it differently, and it went through her like
cold water.

It was not about the salt.

It was about the register. Two years inside the line and the ground comes off
the book — so a man with a chain and a table and three weeks could give a field
one more year, and then next May he could give it one more year, and the field
would never be inside the line for two consecutive years, and it would never
come off the register at all.

It was not a lie he had told once in 1994 and then been trapped by.

It was a mechanism. He had found the one gap in the rule and he had put his whole
working life through it, one year at a time, thirty-one times, and every year he
had written down the truth first so he would know exactly how much he was
holding back.

Section fourteen was the same shape. Smaller, legal, and the same shape.

She started the engine.

# Nine — Who Asked

She filed the interim figure on the twenty-sixth.

Not the provisional. Not section fourteen. She wrote out the true line on the
board's form with the eleven positions and the total of three thousand nine
hundred and eighty-three point five, and she marked it INTERIM pending the
driving of stake eleven, and she signed it, and she put it through the slot in
the harbour office door at ten past eight in the morning because she did not
want to hand it to Ellen Sarai and watch her read it.

The board wrote to the four farms the same afternoon. That was procedure. She had
known it was procedure and had somehow not pictured it: four envelopes, second
class, the standard wording.

Cobb's arrived on the twenty-eighth.

---

He was at the gate of the long acre when she came down the track, and he had the
letter in his hand, folded once, and he did not wave it at her or raise his voice
or do any of the things she had prepared for on the drive over.

"Eleven acres, Reddin says he's got. And Sowerby's lad's lost his road."

"Yes."

"And this one." Cobb looked out over the field. The wire hummed. The crust lay
white in the furrows of the dead third and the rest of it, the two thirds that
were still green, still had another season in them by any eye that was not
holding a chain. "The whole of it, is it."

"The whole of it. The line runs along the top hedge."

"Along the old post."

"Along the old post."

He nodded slowly, as though she had confirmed something about the weather.

"Well," he said. "It's been coming."

She had expected fury and had got this, and it was worse, and she did not know
what to do with her hands.

"Ivor, I'm sorry —"

"Don't." He said it without heat. "Don't do that. You measured a thing and it
came out how it came out. I'll not have you apologising for arithmetic."

"It doesn't feel like arithmetic."

"No," Cobb agreed. "It never did."

And then he was quiet for a while, long enough that she understood he was
deciding, and she had the sense to wait, which she had learned from her father
and had used on this man twice already and had never once thought about what it
cost him.

"I asked him," Cobb said.

The gull went over. She did not say anything.

"May of ninety-four. He'd walked it and he came up that track with his face like
a wet week and he sat in my kitchen and he told me what the ground was doing and
what the rule was, and he said, Ivor, two years of this and the long acre comes
off the register." Cobb had not looked at her. "I had Peggy in the house with the
cancer and I had a boy of nineteen who wanted the farm and I had eight thousand
pounds of borrowing against that field. And I said to him — "

He stopped. Started again.

"I said, is there anything in it. Anything at all. And he sat there a long while
and he said, there might be a year in it. And I said, then give me the year."

Marla found that she had put her hand on the gate.

"He didn't offer," Cobb said. "You want to be clear on that, because I've had
thirty-one years to be clear on it. Tom Vane did not come to me with a scheme.
He came to tell me the truth and I asked him to hold it, and he went away and
thought about it for three weeks and then he did it. And the next May he did it
again, and I never asked him a second time. I never had to."

"Did the others ask?"

"Reddin, I think. Not Hale. Hale never knew a thing about it in his life." Cobb
turned round at last. "Sowerby knew. Sowerby knew and said nothing, which is the
same as asking, only cheaper."

She stood at the gate of a field that was going to stop being a field, opposite
a man who had asked her father to lie for him and had then signed his name as a
witness on his own ground to the reading that proved it.

"Why did you sign it?" she said. "On the fourteenth. You knew what you were
signing."

"Because you asked me to."

"That's not a reason."

"It's the only one I've got." He put the letter in his coat pocket. "He gave me
thirty-one years I hadn't earned. Peggy died in that house in ninety-seven and
the boy grew up and went to Aberdeen and doesn't want a farm and never did.
Thirty-one years, and I spent about four of them grateful and the rest of them
hoping he'd die before anybody found out."

He said that quite steadily.

"And then he did," Cobb said. "And they sent you."

---

The light was going and neither of them moved.

"There's a thing they can do," Cobb said eventually. "Ellen'll have told you.
Where you can't certify."

"She told me."

"A year." He was not pleading. That was what made it unbearable; he laid it down
the way he laid down everything, as a fact available for inspection. "A year and
I could get the borrowing off it and Sowerby's lad could get an access sorted
with Hale, who'd give it to him, because Hale's not losing anything and Hale's a
decent man. A year and it's four families managing something instead of four
families being done to."

"And next May?"

"Next May you'd file it true and I'd have no argument left. I'd have had my
year." He looked at her. "I'm asking. I've not asked you for anything in your
life and I'm asking for this."

She stood there with the wire humming and the crust white in the furrows and the
old post standing at the top hedge where it had stood since before she could
walk, saying the true thing, in plain sight, for thirty-one years, while every
May a decent man wrote down something else.

"I know you are," she said.

She drove home the long way and did not turn the van around at any point, and
that felt like nothing at all.

# Ten — The Hearing

They held it in the town hall because the harbour board's office had four chairs,
and thirty-one people came, which for a Thursday evening in May was most of the
coast.

It was not a trial. She had to keep telling herself that. It was a scheduled
meeting of the harbour board with an item on the agenda, and the item was
*Coastal Survey 2026: interim filing*, and the chairman read it out in the voice
people use for agendas.

Ellen Sarai took her through it. Dates, instrument, method, the eleven positions,
the total. Marla answered in the flat way she had answered questions since she
was nineteen, and it went well enough, and then Sarai turned a page.

"On the eighteenth of May you returned the board's theodolite and completed the
instrument log. Outcome of verification."

"Yes."

"Would you read what you wrote."

Marla looked at the photocopy in front of her. Her own handwriting, two inches
wide, ruled in blue.

"'Reading confirmed.'"

"Is that a true statement?"

"Yes."

"Is it a complete one?"

The hall was very quiet. Somebody's chair creaked at the back.

"No," Marla said.

Sarai nodded once and made a note, and Marla understood — not resentfully, with
something closer to gratitude — that she had just been made to say it out loud in
front of the town, on the record, eight days before it could be said about her by
somebody else.

"Thank you," Sarai said. "The board should note that the surveyor disclosed the
discrepancy to this office on the twenty-fourth of May, unprompted, and filed an
interim figure on the twenty-sixth."

---

The chairman set out section fourteen.

He did it badly, which helped, because it made the thing sound like the piece of
administrative machinery it was rather than the mercy everybody in the room knew
it to be. Unable to certify. Stake eleven not driven. Provisional filing carries
the previous figure. Twelve months.

"It is entirely proper," he said. "It is not a device."

"It's not," Marla said. "I want that on the record too. Nobody in this room has
asked me to file a false figure."

Nobody moved.

"That's true," the chairman said, after a moment. "Nobody has."

Hale stood up.

He was a big slow man who had never spoken at anything in his life as far as
Marla knew, and he stood with his cap in his hands and looked at a spot about
four feet up the wall behind the board table.

"I don't lose anything," he said. "That's been made clear to me. My ground's
outside it and I want that said, because I'd not want anybody thinking I was
speaking for myself."

He turned the cap over.

"Jenny told me the wet corner's safe. And I've been sat here two days thinking
about that and what I keep coming back to is — I never knew. Thirty years I never
knew, and it turns out I was the only one of the four of us that didn't, and the
reason I didn't know is that I never needed to."

He stopped. Started again, more slowly.

"So I don't think I get a vote on the year. Them that got the thirty-one years
should say. But I'd not want it done in the dark again. Whatever's done. That's
all."

He sat down.

Cobb did not stand up. He was three rows back with his hands on his knees and he
did not stand up and did not speak, and when Marla looked at him he met her eye
and shook his head very slightly, once, and she understood that he had asked her
at the gate on the twenty-eighth and was not going to ask her again in front of
thirty-one people, because that would be leaning, and Ivor Cobb did not lean.

---

The chairman said the board would receive her filing by the first of June in
whichever form she certified, and asked whether she had anything further.

"One thing." Marla stood. "If I file the true line, I want to enter a document
with it. My father's field book for 1994."

Sarai's pen stopped.

"On what grounds," the chairman said.

"On the grounds that the 1994 recorded position is the baseline every subsequent
survey has been measured against, and it's wrong, and the correct figures for
that year exist and are in my possession, in his handwriting." She kept her voice
level with an effort she hoped was not visible. "You can't understand a
hundred-and-twenty-eight-metre correction without them. It looks like an error.
It isn't one."

"You understand what entering that would do."

"Yes."

"To his name."

"Yes," Marla said. "I understand exactly what it would do."

The chairman looked at Sarai, and Sarai looked at the table, and then she said,
without looking up: "It's admissible. It's a survey record. It should have been
filed in 1994."

The hall was quiet again, differently.

"Then the board will receive it," the chairman said, "if it is submitted."

*If it is submitted.*

She walked out into the square with two days left and both doors standing open
behind her, and neither of them locked, and nobody at her back pushing her
towards either one.

# Eleven — The Thirty-First of May

She drove stake eleven at seven in the morning on the thirty-first, in the rain,
with nobody watching.

It went in at the top hedge beside the old post, forty metres landward of where
it had stood since 1994, and she squared the head and cut the date and stood back
from it, and the survey was complete. Eleven stakes. A certifiable line. Section
fourteen closed behind her with a click she could almost hear.

She had not decided anything. That was the part she would try to explain later
and never manage. She had woken at five and lain there and then got up and put
her boots on, and by the time she was hammering she understood that the decision
had been made somewhere below the level where she did her thinking, and that all
that remained was to find out what it was.

---

The board office opened at nine.

She filed the true line: three thousand nine hundred and eighty-three point five
metres of defended frontage, eleven positions, dated and certified, one day
before the deadline.

And with it she filed the field book.

She had thought about how to do it for two days and in the end she did it the
way he would have: plainly, with a covering note in her own hand, no adjectives.

*Submitted with the 2026 survey: field book, T. Vane, May 1994. It contains two
surveys of the same coast. The first, completed 14 May 1994, is a true record of
the salt line as it then stood. The second, filed 3 June 1994, is not. The
discrepancy at stake eleven is 40.0 metres. Every survey filed between 1995 and
2025 was measured from the second figure and is therefore wrong by an amount
which increases through the record.*

*The true 1994 position is marked on the ground by an unmarked witness post at
the top hedge of the long acre, which has stood there throughout.*

*Thomas Vane recorded the correct figures every year of his working life and
retained them. Thirty-one field books are held at the cottage. I have not
inspected all of them. They are available to the board.*

*The reason for the discrepancy is stated in the record of the hearing of 29 May
and I do not repeat it here. It was not committed for gain.*

*M. Vane, Surveyor. 31 May.*

Ellen Sarai read it twice, standing up, and then sat down and read it a third
time.

"He kept them," she said.

"He kept all of them."

"Thirty-one years." Sarai put the note down very carefully, as if it might mark.
"You know what this does."

"It puts his name on it."

"It puts his name on it in his own handwriting, in a public record, permanently."
Sarai looked at her. "There was a version where you filed the true line and said
nothing about 1994. It would have looked like a correction. Sloppy predecessor,
accumulated drift. Nobody would have dug."

"I know."

"Why not that one?"

Marla had thought she would find this hard to say and discovered that she did not.

"Because it would have been the same thing he did. A true number and a false
reason. He spent thirty-one years filing a figure and keeping the truth in a
press upstairs where it did nobody any good." She looked at the note on the
counter. "If I'd filed the line and buried the book I'd have been keeping a
press of my own."

Sarai was quiet for a moment.

"He'd have hated this," she said. "You understand that. He'd have hated it very
much."

"He kept them," Marla said. "For thirty-one years, in order, in a house he knew
I'd inherit, along with a job he knew I'd take. He could have burned them any
autumn of his life."

She picked up her copy of the receipt.

"He didn't leave me a confession. He left me a survey. I've filed it."

---

Outside it had stopped raining and the light had come up off the water the way it
does after, flat and very clear, and the whole coast was visible all the way to
Sanders Point.

She stood on the step of the harbour office and looked at it.

Somewhere along that line four families were going to open letters that were
already written, and a pier was going to be recosted, and an insurance schedule
was going to reprice to a number nobody had ever tested, and a man who had been
dead five months was going to be discussed in that town for a long time in a way
he had spent his whole life making sure he would not be.

And the line on the ground was true.

That was all she had. It did not feel like enough and she understood, standing
there with the receipt in her hand, that it was not supposed to.

# Twelve — The Fourth of June

They drove the line on the fourth of June, which was a Thursday, low water at
five to six in the morning.

She had expected to do it alone. She was at Kell Head at half five with the
stakes in the barrow and the chain over her shoulder and there was a truck parked
up on the road with its lights off, and Ivor Cobb got out of it and came down
the shingle without saying anything and picked up the other end of the chain.

"You don't have to do this."

"No," Cobb agreed, and did not put it down.

They worked north up the coast. Stake one, two, three. He held the chain and
read the figures back to her when she called them, which he had done for her
father for thirty years and had never once done for her, and by stake five they
had found the rhythm of it and stopped talking except about the work.

Reddin came out at the seaward strip and stood at his fence with his hands in his
pockets and watched them set stake three at its true place, eleven acres inside
his boundary, and when they had done it he lifted his chin at her about a
centimetre and went back up to the house. That was all of it. She thought that
might be all of it forever, and it was more than she had earned.

Sowerby's lad brought them tea in a flask at nine and did not stay.

Hale was not there. Hale had gone to Aberdeen for his grandson's birthday, which
Jenny had told her in the square with a slightly defiant look, and Marla had said
that sounded lovely and had meant it.

---

The long acre took the longest because it was the one they both wanted to be
over.

Stake eleven was already in — she had driven it on the thirty-first, in the rain,
and it stood at the top hedge beside the old post with the date cut into its head.
There was nothing to do there but check it.

She ran the chain out and confirmed the position and wrote it in the book, and
Cobb stood at the fence looking down over the whole field, the two green thirds
and the dead one, all of it now outside the register, all of it now just ground.

"The barley'd have come up all right this year," he said.

"It would."

"Aye." He shifted his weight off the bad hip. "Well."

That was the whole of what he said about it, then or ever.

He helped her carry the barrow back up the track, and at the top he stopped and
looked at the old post, and after a while he said:

"He put that in the same afternoon. Ninety-four. Before he ever came up to the
house."

Marla stopped.

"Before?"

"Before." Cobb's face did not change. "He'd walked the line and he'd put his
posts in at the true places, all the way up, and then he came and sat in my
kitchen. And after — after I asked him, and after he'd thought about it — he went
back and pulled every one of them out and put them where he put them." He
nodded slowly at the pale weathered post at the top hedge. "Except that one."

The wire hummed. Out on the shingle a gull went over and did not call.

"He left it," Cobb said. "One post, out of eleven, standing in the right place
where anybody with a chain could find it. And he never mentioned it once in
thirty-one years and neither did I."

---

She went back to the cottage in the afternoon and the press room was cold and the
thirty-one books were where they had always been.

She had not looked at the 1994 book since she filed it, because it was not hers
any more; it was in the board's cabinet in a building with a door that locked.
But there were thirty others, and she had not gone through them properly, and now
she sat on the floor and did.

They were all the same. The true survey, then the filed one. Weather, tides, a
gate hinge, a shopping list. He had not been a diarist.

In the 1979 book — the earliest, the one before he had the job, when he was
carrying the chain for somebody else — the first page said, in a much younger
hand:

*T. Vane, assisting. Surveyor: V. Reid.*

She sat with that for a while.

Then she took the brass lighter out of her jacket pocket, where it had been since
the twelfth of May, and turned it over and looked at the initials she had never
once looked at in her whole life until the morning she started walking a line
that was not true.

She put it back in her pocket. She would find out or she would not. There were
thirty books to read and a coast to walk again next May, and it seemed to her,
sitting on the floor of a cold room in her father's house, that she had a long
time to be careful in.

---

The letter from the board came at the end of the month.

It thanked her for the 2026 survey and confirmed the filed figure of three
thousand nine hundred and eighty-three point five metres. It noted that the
question of the 1994 record had been referred and that she would be informed. It
observed, in a separate paragraph, that the annual appointment was made at the
board's discretion and was not hereditary.

Then it appointed her for 2027.

She read it standing at the window with the fields going down grey and green to
the water, and the salt line out there somewhere in the crust and the dying grass,
moving landward at a metre a year, the way it always had, patiently, without any
interest at all in what anybody wrote down about it.

She put the letter on the table, on top of the box.

Next May, she thought. Twelfth.
